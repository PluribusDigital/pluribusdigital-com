On January 26, the President of the United States issued an executive order mandating zero trust cybersecurity principles across federal agencies. The core idea behind zero trust is simple to state and hard to implement: no system or application should assume that any other system or application it talks to hasn't been compromised.

That shift touches nearly everything about how federal IT security has traditionally worked.

## What changes

- **It affects every employee and contractor.** Login and application security across government will need to move to a zero trust model — this isn't limited to a single agency or system.
- **VPNs are on their way out.** Under zero trust, users authenticate directly to the specific websites and applications they need using Single Sign-On (SSO), and each service is hardened to be safely exposed on the internet rather than hidden behind a perimeter.
- **Multi-factor authentication becomes standard, not optional.** MFA has been common in the private sector for years; this order makes it a core, expected technology across government systems as well.

## Why it matters

Zero trust represents a real departure from the perimeter-based security model most federal systems were built around — the assumption that anything "inside the network" can be trusted. Instead, every request gets verified, every time, regardless of where it originates.

For teams building and maintaining government systems, that means rethinking identity, access management, and network architecture together, rather than treating them as separate concerns.
